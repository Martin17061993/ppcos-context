Combined executive summary

Client: Stay Cold Apparel Date: 2026-08-07 Vertical: D2C e-commerce (apparel) Account: 359-911-6618
Requires a decision today

Spend is running at roughly 2.7× the monthly target, and the ramp started six days ago

The account spent about €650/day through mid-July. On 31 July that became €1,476, and the first five days of August ran €2,378 → €2,948 → €3,160 → €3,274 → €2,530. Month-to-date is €14,290; the month-end projection is €78,000–93,000 against a €32,500 target.

For calibration: the June 2026 over-scaling — documented internally as the most expensive decision period of the first half-year, where the marginal return fell to a 67-cent loss per euro spent — peaked at €62,000/month. The current daily rate is above that.

The single question that matters: was this ramp authorised? If yes, it needs monitoring against the clean numbers, not the reported ones — and those are now measured exactly, so the monitoring is straightforward. If no, it needs stopping. Nothing else in this report should be acted on before that answer.

The one-paragraph version

Three audits, run the same day, converge on one story. The account is well built and badly instrumented, and it is now spending fast into that blind spot. Structure, settings and ad-group hygiene are genuinely good — location targeting, exclusion method, ad rotation, tracking templates and Display-Network hygiene are clean across all 15 enabled campaigns, there are no shared-budget conflicts, no single-keyword ad groups and no ad groups missing ads. What is broken is the measurement, and what is urgent is the spend.

Root cause one: conversion value reaching the bidding algorithm is inflated by exactly 1.51× — now measured, no longer estimated. The twelfth audit settled it. 14 of the 15 enabled campaigns override the account’s clean default conversion goal with a custom goal containing a second, non-primary purchase action. Over the last full 30-day window the reported value series is fed by exactly two actions: the real purchase event at €282,627 and that second action at €143,297. The one campaign that does not override — Search TOF — carries no inflation at all, which confirms the mechanism from first principles rather than by inference. The consequence is not abstract: the largest campaign in the account, Shopping FOKUSPRODUKTE at €12,316, reports a healthy 3.12 return but is actually at 1.71 against a 1.9 break-even — it loses money on every euro. Two of the three automated scoring engines classified it as profitable and would have recommended increasing its budget. Those verdicts had to be overridden by hand, and the measurement now proves the overrides were right. One earlier suspicion is disproven: an enabled value rule adding +€42.20 per conversion was flagged as a possible third inflation layer. It is not — both contributing actions average roughly €140 per conversion, essentially the €143 average order value, so the 1.51 factor is fully explained without it.

Root cause two: the July budget work broke the account's own rules. FOKUSPRODUKTE was raised €550 → €1,150/day (+109%) in a single day, in two steps three minutes apart. Search TOF doubled over seven days. PROSPECTING rose 42% — on top of the increase that had already been measured at −27% and logged internally as the most expensive decision of the half-year. The documented guardrail is ±30% per step, seven days apart, and increases above +90% have a track record of nought for six. August carries no promo window on file, so there is no seasonal justification recorded.

What is emphatically not the problem: competitors. The competitive audit — the strongest score of the four at 73 — found that across 90 days not a single campaign lost impression share. Brand visibility sits at 84–96% and is stable. The one campaign whose cost-per-click rose sharply simultaneously gained 11.7 points of share, which is the signature of an advertiser bidding deeper, not a competitor bidding harder. Rising costs in this account are self-inflicted.

And the competitive audit found the cheapest fix available. The non-brand campaigns lose 90–93% of available impressions, almost entirely to ad rank. Of the six non-brand keywords carrying a Quality Score, all six show landing page experience as BELOW AVERAGE — while ad relevance is ABOVE AVERAGE on five of six and expected click-through rate is fine on all six. The ads are good; the destination pages are what cap the score at 4–7 and keep the campaigns off the page. Brand keywords, pointing at different pages, score a perfect 10. Fixing landing page experience is the only lever that recovers visibility without spending anything, and it lifts every non-brand keyword simultaneously. Internal documentation already flagged the "PDP truth gap" as an open risk and described it, word for word, as a "Quality Score driver" — this is the confirmation. The thirteenth audit then proved it outright. Every ad in the account — brand and non-brand alike — sends traffic to the same homepage. Google rates that page above average for brand searches and below average for non-brand searches, on 19 keywords and 9 keywords respectively, with no exception in either direction. Same page, opposite verdicts. A slow or broken page would penalise both equally; only a relevance problem can split that cleanly.

A quieter finding: someone is rebuilding the Demand Gen and Video stack right now — 113 change-log entries yesterday and 38 today, concentrated on paused campaigns including one previously profiled as healthy at 5.78 return and left idle. Likely good news, but it needs budget that is not in the current plan, and a known tracking fault must be disabled first or it will corrupt bidding signal from day one.

Nothing here is fixed by spending more. Everything here is configuration, governance, and one landing page.

Critical issues across all three audits

Ordered by business impact, not by diagnostic severity.

Search terms · ST-D01 — read this before cutting anything
Nearly a third of spend looks wasted. Only about a tenth of it actually is.
An automated scan of 678,000 search queries flags 31.7% of spend as irrelevant — roughly €18,200. Broken down, that number falls apart: 57% of it sits on core product terms (hoodie, shirt, tattoo, punk, goth, metal), which are the categories the business exists to sell. Another €2,100 is competitor brand names, which internal policy explicitly protects because those shoppers convert — and this run proved it: two competitor terms returned 10 and 11 conversions at three to six times the target return. A further €479 is the company's own brand name leaking into non-brand campaigns, which should be redirected, never blocked.

Genuinely wasteful spend is about €5,200 — roughly 9%, concentrated in adjacent-but-wrong categories (activewear, techwear, swimwear), general retailers and other artists' merchandise. Acting on the headline figure would have cut the front door of the business.

The single most useful sentence in this report

The offer scores 97 out of 100 with no failures at all — the strongest asset in the business. The page that sells it to cold traffic scores 56. So the landing page problem is not a content-creation problem: the language, the proof and the scarcity mechanics already exist and are already documented. Named tattoo artists with verifiable credits, weight specs in every product title, prints tested to 200 washes, 4.6 out of 5 from 6,477 reviews, genuinely sold-out drops. None of it reaches someone searching for a punk hoodie. Fixing this is a deployment job, not a creative one.

Landing page · LP-D15 — Critical · ROOT CAUSE
The words the campaigns buy do not appear on the page they send people to
The non-brand campaign bids on punk, goth, metal and rocker searches. On the page those clicks land on, each of those four words appears exactly zero times — across 1,716 words of copy. The headline reads "Armoured in confidence, I am unstoppable" and the hero product is an oversized t-shirt. The correlation with quality scoring is exact: the only subculture word that does appear is "tattoo", five times including the page title — and the two tattoo keywords are the only ones scoring 7 out of 10. Every punk, goth and metal keyword scores 4 or 5. That capped score is what produces the 64% of impressions lost to ad rank and the sub-break-even returns on €1,961 of keyword spend.

And the obvious fix does not exist yet. Of the 32 collection pages on the site, not one is organised around punk, goth, metal or tattoo — they are built by product type, drop, bestseller window and artist collaboration. There is no better page to point at. The fix is to add that language to the existing category pages, or build pages that carry it. Two collections already named "featured collection prospecting" and "featured collection retargeting" suggest someone saw this coming.

Placements · PL-D05 — High
A 38,000-entry brand-safety list was built, then attached to nothing that runs
The account contains a serious exclusion list — 38,208 entries covering 24,553 mobile apps, 7,759 YouTube channels, 5,757 placements and 139 of the 140 app categories Google offers. Real work went into it. It is attached to exactly three campaigns, and all three are removed or paused. None of the seven campaigns currently serving placements is covered. Meanwhile Performance Max is placing three million impressions across 20,395 placements, 3,301 of them inside mobile apps, with no exclusion coverage and only one content label blocked account-wide.

Important: no money is being wasted here. Total flagged spend is €118, all of it historical from one paused campaign. This is brand exposure on unvetted surfaces, not budget leakage — but for a brand whose whole proposition is aesthetic, that is worth fixing. The fix is also cheap: the exclusion material already exists and simply needs promoting to account level, where it will apply to Performance Max. And it should happen before the Demand Gen rebuild currently in progress goes live.

A near-miss worth recording

An intermediate run of this measurement, executed without an explicit date filter, silently returned the account’s entire history instead of 30 days. It showed six conversion actions feeding the value series and put Search TOF’s inflation at 2.62 — a figure that would have invalidated eleven audits and reversed two headline conclusions in this report. It was caught by one sanity check: the implied conversion value exceeded what the campaign had actually spent, by twenty times. Re-run correctly, the true figure is 1.00. The discarded result was not worthless either — it independently rediscovered a documented historical period when two analytics systems counted the same purchases in parallel, confirming that record too.

Where the audits disagree — and which one is right

The bidding audit flagged rising cost-per-click on the two largest campaigns as a possible competitive-pressure signal. The competitive audit checked and found the opposite: the campaign whose cost rose 47% simultaneously gained 11.7 points of impression share. Competitors are not bidding harder — this account is. The cost increase is real and it damages profitability, but the cause is the budget and bid changes of 17–24 July, not the market. Treating it as a competitive event would point at entirely the wrong response.

Five automated verdicts were overridden by hand

All five for the same reason: the scoring engines compare a clean break-even threshold of 1.9 against the platform's inflated value series. Left uncorrected, they rated the account's largest money-loser as profitable and would have recommended increasing its budget. Two bidding verdicts and three budget verdicts were adjusted, each documented in the underlying reports with its reasoning. This is the practical cost of the measurement fault: it does not merely obscure performance, it inverts automated recommendations. The twelfth audit has since verified every factor those overrides relied on — measured 1.82 against a documented 1.81 on FOKUSPRODUKTE, 1.79 against 1.88 on PROSPECTING, 1.90 against 1.93 on DSA. The manual corrections were right.
